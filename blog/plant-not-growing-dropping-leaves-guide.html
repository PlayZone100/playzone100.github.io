Why Is My Plant Not Growing or Dropping Leaves? Complete Guide (2026)

No new leaves for months, or leaves falling with no obvious sign of pests or disease — here are the 9 real causes, from the obvious to the easy-to-miss.

A plant that stalls — no new leaves, no visible growth, maybe some leaves dropping with no obvious spots, pests, or discoloration — is one of the more frustrating houseplant problems precisely because nothing looks dramatically wrong. There's no crispy edge or yellow blotch to point to. The causes tend to be more about the plant's overall environment and conditions than any single visible symptom, so this guide works a little differently: rather than matching a symptom to a cause, work through these roughly in order, since the first few are by far the most common.

1. It's simply the wrong season

This is the most overlooked cause, and often the correct answer. Most houseplants, even tropical ones grown indoors, follow a seasonal growth rhythm shaped by their native climate: active growth in spring and summer when light and temperature both increase, and a natural slowdown or full dormancy in fall and winter.

What it looks like: a plant that grew steadily through spring and summer, then simply stopped producing new leaves as days got shorter, without any other sign of stress — no yellowing, no wilting, no pests. The plant otherwise looks perfectly healthy, just still.

The fix: nothing needed. Reduce watering and stop or pause fertilizing during this period, since a resting plant uses much less of both. Growth typically resumes on its own in spring as light levels rise again.

2. Not enough light

Light is the fuel for growth — without enough of it, a plant can survive for a long time but has little energy to spare for producing new leaves. This is extremely common with plants placed for their look in a room rather than for the actual light conditions there.

What it looks like: slow or stalled growth, new leaves noticeably smaller than older ones, long gaps between nodes (leggy, stretched-out growth as the plant reaches for more light), and leaves leaning or growing only toward a light source.

The fix:

  • Move the plant closer to a window, or to a brighter window if the current one doesn't match the plant's needs — check the specific light requirements for your plant species rather than guessing.
  • Rotate the pot regularly so growth doesn't lean permanently toward one side.
  • Consider a grow light for spaces without enough natural light, especially through winter when daylight hours are shortest.

3. Transplant or relocation shock

Plants are more sensitive to sudden change than they look. Repotting, moving to a new home, a big shift in light or temperature, or even a long car ride can trigger a stress response where the plant pauses growth and sometimes sheds leaves while it adjusts.

What it looks like: leaf drop or stalled growth beginning shortly after a specific change — a move, a repot, a new location in the house — in a plant that was previously doing fine.

This is usually temporary. Most plants recover within 1-3 weeks once they adjust to their new conditions, without needing any special treatment beyond consistent, normal care.

The fix: keep care steady and predictable during the adjustment period — avoid the temptation to add extra water or fertilizer to "help." Give it time, and avoid moving the plant again if you can help it during this window.

4. Pot-bound roots

As covered in more depth in our root rot guide, roots that have completely filled a pot lose their ability to take up water and nutrients efficiently, which directly limits how much new growth the plant can produce.

What it looks like: stalled growth combined with roots visible through drainage holes, roots circling on the soil surface, or the plant needing water unusually often because there's more root than soil left to hold moisture.

The fix: repot into a container 1-2 inches (2-5 cm) larger in diameter with fresh soil, gently loosening circled roots so they can grow outward.

5. Nutrient deficiency

Potting soil has a limited nutrient supply that depletes over time, especially in a plant that's been in the same soil for over a year without fertilizing. Without replenishment, growth slows simply because the plant lacks the raw materials to build new tissue.

What it looks like: slow, weak new growth, sometimes with pale or slightly yellow new leaves (different from the older-leaves-only pattern of natural aging), in a plant that hasn't been fertilized or repotted in a long time.

The fix: feed with a balanced houseplant fertilizer during the active growing season, following label dilution rates, or refresh the soil entirely with a repot if it's been more than 1-2 years.

6. Overwatering

Chronically overwatered soil, even before it progresses to full root rot, keeps roots in a low-oxygen environment where they function poorly — the plant effectively can't take up what it needs to grow, even though water isn't the thing it's short on.

What it looks like: stalled growth combined with soil that stays wet for many days after watering, a slightly sour smell from the soil, or leaves that feel unusually soft. See our root rot guide for the full early-warning signs.

The fix: let soil dry appropriately between waterings for the specific plant, and check that the pot drains well.

7. Temperature stress

Most common houseplants grow best in a fairly narrow comfortable range, generally similar to what feels comfortable to people — roughly 65-80°F (18-27°C). Consistently cold rooms, drafty windows, or a spot too close to a heating vent can all suppress growth even when light and water are otherwise fine.

What it looks like: stalled growth in a plant kept in a consistently cold room, near a drafty window, or right next to a heat source, without other obvious symptoms.

The fix: move the plant to a spot with more stable, moderate temperatures, away from single-pane glass in winter and away from direct heat sources.

8. Old, compacted, or depleted soil

Potting soil breaks down physically over time, becoming denser and holding less air even if nutrients were replenished with fertilizer. Compacted soil restricts root growth and oxygen availability much like being pot-bound does, even in an appropriately sized pot.

What it looks like: water running through the pot quickly without being absorbed, soil that looks dense and lacks the light, airy texture of fresh mix, and stalled growth despite otherwise normal care in a plant that hasn't been repotted in 2+ years.

The fix: refresh with new potting mix, even if the current pot size is still appropriate — this alone often restarts stalled growth within a few weeks.

9. Natural leaf cycling

As covered in our brown leaves guide, plants continuously shed their oldest leaves as part of normal turnover. A small, steady rate of leaf drop from the bottom of an otherwise actively growing plant isn't a problem.

What it looks like: occasional single leaves dropping from the base of the plant, while new growth continues to appear at a normal pace elsewhere.

The fix: none needed, unless the rate of drop increases noticeably or spreads beyond the oldest leaves.

FAQ

Is it normal for a plant to drop a few leaves after I bring it home?

Yes. Losing a handful of leaves in the first 1-2 weeks after a move, repotting, or a change in light is a very common stress response called transplant shock, and most plants recover on their own once they adjust. It only becomes a concern if leaf drop continues well beyond that window or affects more than a small portion of the plant.

How long should I wait before worrying my plant has stopped growing?

It depends on the season. In fall and winter, most houseplants naturally slow down or pause growth entirely, and that's not a problem on its own. If a plant shows no new growth for 2-3 months during its active growing season, typically spring through summer, that's a reasonable point to start checking light, roots, and feeding.

Can a plant recover after dropping most of its leaves?

Often, yes, as long as the stem and roots are still healthy. Many houseplants can regrow a full set of leaves from a bare stem once the underlying cause is fixed, though it can take several weeks to months depending on the plant and season. Check the stem for firmness and the roots for rot before assuming the plant is done for.

Does a bigger pot make a plant grow faster?

Not necessarily, and it can backfire. A pot much larger than the current root ball holds more soil and moisture than the plant is using, which increases the risk of root rot rather than speeding up growth. Sizing up gradually, by about 1-2 inches (2-5 cm) in diameter at a time, supports steady growth without that risk.
