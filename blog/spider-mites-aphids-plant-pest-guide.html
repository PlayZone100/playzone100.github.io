Spider Mites and Aphids on Houseplants: How to Identify and Treat Them (2026)

Fine webbing, sticky leaves, or tiny specks that move — here's how to tell which pest you're dealing with and how to get rid of it for good.

Most houseplant pest problems come down to a short list of usual suspects, and each one leaves a fairly distinct calling card — a certain kind of web, a certain kind of residue, a certain size and shape of bug. Once you know what to look for, identifying which pest you're dealing with usually takes less than a minute with a magnifying glass or even just a close look with your phone's camera zoomed in.

Quick check: which pest do you have?

  • Fine, spider-web-like threads between leaves or in leaf joints, tiny specks that move: spider mites
  • Small, soft-bodied bugs clustered on new growth or flower buds, sticky residue on leaves: aphids
  • White, cottony fluff in leaf joints or along stems: mealybugs
  • Small brown or tan bumps stuck to stems or leaf undersides that don't move: scale insects
  • Tiny flies hovering near the soil surface, especially after watering: fungus gnats

Spider mites

Spider mites are not insects but tiny arachnids, so small that individually they look like moving dust specks to the naked eye. They thrive in warm, dry conditions — exactly what most centrally heated homes provide in winter — and feed by piercing plant cells and sucking out the contents, which is what causes their telltale damage pattern.

Signs: fine, silky webbing in leaf joints, along stems, or across the undersides of leaves; tiny pale yellow or white speckling (called stippling) across the leaf surface where cells have been fed on; leaves eventually turning bronze, yellow, or dull and dry looking. Shake or tap an affected leaf over a white sheet of paper — if you see tiny specks fall and start crawling, that confirms mites.

Treatment:

  • Isolate the plant immediately, since mites spread quickly to nearby plants.
  • Rinse the plant thoroughly under running water, focusing on leaf undersides, to physically knock off as many mites and webs as possible.
  • Apply insecticidal soap or neem oil, coating all leaf surfaces including undersides, and repeat every 5-7 days for at least 2-3 rounds, since treatments kill active mites but not eggs.
  • Raise humidity around the plant going forward — mites struggle to thrive above roughly 50% humidity, so this helps prevent recurrence.

Aphids

Aphids are small, soft-bodied insects, usually green, black, or brown, that cluster in groups on tender new growth, stems, and flower buds where plant tissue is softest and easiest to feed on.

Signs: visible clusters of small bugs on new growth or buds; a sticky, shiny residue on leaves called honeydew, which is aphid excrement and can attract ants or develop black sooty mold; curled, distorted, or stunted new growth.

Treatment:

  • For light infestations, simply wipe or rinse the aphids off by hand under running water.
  • For larger infestations, apply insecticidal soap or neem oil directly onto the clusters, repeating every 5-7 days until they're gone.
  • Wipe off sticky honeydew residue with a damp cloth to prevent sooty mold from developing.
  • Check nearby plants, since aphids spread and reproduce quickly once established.

Mealybugs

Mealybugs are soft-bodied insects that cover themselves in a white, waxy, cotton-like coating, making them one of the more visually distinctive houseplant pests once you know what you're looking at.

Signs: small white cottony masses tucked into leaf joints, along stems, or on the undersides of leaves; sticky honeydew residue similar to aphids; yellowing or wilting in heavily affected areas.

Treatment:

  • Dab visible mealybugs directly with a cotton swab dipped in rubbing alcohol, which dissolves their waxy coating and kills them on contact — effective for light infestations.
  • For larger infestations, follow up with insecticidal soap or neem oil applied thoroughly, repeated every 7-10 days for several rounds, since mealybugs also lay eggs that survive the first treatment.
  • Check leaf joints and the underside of leaves carefully, as mealybugs favor these hidden, protected spots.

Scale insects

Scale insects attach themselves to a stem or leaf and stay put, protected by a hard or waxy shell-like covering that makes them look more like small bumps or blemishes than living bugs — which is exactly why they're often mistaken for a plant disease rather than a pest at first glance.

Signs: small brown, tan, or gray bumps stuck firmly to stems or leaf undersides that don't move and can be difficult to scrape off; sticky honeydew residue; yellowing leaves and general decline if left untreated.

Treatment:

  • Scrape off visible scale with a fingernail or soft toothbrush, or dab with rubbing alcohol on a cotton swab, since their hard shell makes topical sprays less effective on adults.
  • Follow up with neem oil or insecticidal soap to catch the young, mobile "crawler" stage, which is more vulnerable than adult scale.
  • Repeat treatment every 1-2 weeks over several weeks, since scale has an extended life cycle and one treatment rarely gets everything.

Fungus gnats

Fungus gnats are small, dark, mosquito-like flies that are more of a nuisance and a sign of overly wet soil than a direct threat to a mature, healthy plant, though their larvae can damage the roots of seedlings and young plants.

Signs: tiny flies hovering near the soil surface or flying up when the pot is disturbed, especially noticeable after watering; small white larvae visible in the top layer of soil if you look closely.

Treatment:

  • Let the top inch or two (2-5 cm) of soil dry out fully between waterings, since fungus gnats need consistently moist soil to breed.
  • Use yellow sticky traps placed near the soil surface to catch adult gnats and interrupt their breeding cycle.
  • Add a thin layer of sand or diatomaceous earth on top of the soil, which makes it harder for adults to lay eggs and for larvae to survive near the surface.

General treatment steps that work for most pests

  1. Isolate the affected plant away from others as soon as you spot a pest, to limit spread.
  2. Physically remove as many pests as you can by rinsing, wiping, or scraping before applying anything else.
  3. Apply insecticidal soap or neem oil thoroughly, covering leaf undersides and stems, not just the tops of leaves where damage is most visible.
  4. Repeat on a schedule — most pests need 2-4 treatments spaced 5-10 days apart to also catch eggs and newly hatched young that the first round missed.
  5. Recheck weekly for a few weeks after the infestation appears gone, since a small surviving population can rebuild quickly.

Preventing pests from coming back

  • Inspect new plants closely, including leaf undersides, before bringing them near your existing collection.
  • Quarantine new plants for 1-2 weeks before placing them with other plants.
  • Keep humidity in a healthy range for your specific plants — very dry air favors spider mites in particular.
  • Avoid overwatering, which creates the moist soil conditions fungus gnats need to breed.
  • Wipe down leaves periodically, which also gives you a chance to spot early signs before an infestation takes hold.

FAQ

How do I know if my plant has spider mites or just dust?

Wipe the leaf with a damp white cloth or tissue. Dust wipes away clean. Spider mites leave tiny reddish or brown smear marks, and you'll often also see very fine webbing in leaf joints or the undersides of leaves, which dust never produces.

Can houseplant pests spread to my other plants?

Yes, most common houseplant pests spread easily to nearby plants, especially if leaves are touching. Isolating a newly affected plant away from the rest of your collection as soon as you spot a pest is one of the most effective single steps you can take.

Do I need to throw away an infested plant?

Rarely. Most infestations can be treated successfully with isolation, physical removal, and repeated insecticidal soap or neem oil applications. Discarding is usually only necessary if an infestation is severe, has been present for months untreated, or keeps returning despite consistent treatment.

Are natural or home remedies effective against plant pests?

Insecticidal soap and neem oil, both widely available and commonly considered natural options, are effective against most common houseplant pests when applied thoroughly and repeated on schedule. A strong stream of water to physically knock pests off also helps as a supporting step, though it rarely works alone for a full infestation.
