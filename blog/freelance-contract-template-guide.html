Freelance Contract Template: What to Include and How to Write One (2026 Guide)

Updated September 20, 2026 · 12-minute read

Every freelancer has a story about the project that went wrong. The client who kept adding "small" requests. The invoice that stayed unpaid for four months. The logo that ended up on a competitor's website. Almost all of these stories share one root cause: there was no clear written agreement, or the agreement was too vague to help.

A good freelance contract does not make you look distrustful. It makes you look professional. It tells the client exactly what they are buying, when they will receive it, what it costs, and what happens if plans change. In this guide you will learn what a freelance contract template should contain, how it differs from a proposal, which mistakes cost freelancers the most money, and how to produce clean documents quickly instead of rewriting them from scratch for every client.

Note: this article is general information for freelancers and small businesses, not legal advice. Contract law varies by country and state, so have a qualified lawyer review your final template, especially for high-value projects.

Why every freelancer needs a written contract

A verbal agreement or a few chat messages can technically be enough to form a contract in many places. In practice, they are almost impossible to rely on when there is a disagreement. Memories differ, messages are scattered across apps, and nobody remembers whether the client approved two rounds of revisions or five.

A written contract does four things for you:

  • Sets expectations. Client and freelancer read the same description of the work before it starts.
  • Protects your income. Payment amounts, deadlines and late fees are agreed in advance, not negotiated after the work is done.
  • Limits scope creep. Anything outside the agreed deliverables becomes a paid change, not a free favour.
  • Builds trust. Clients, especially international ones, take you more seriously when you send a structured document rather than an email with a price.

Proposal vs. contract: what is the difference?

Many new freelancers mix these up. A proposal is a sales document. It explains the client's problem, describes your approach, shows your price and persuades them to choose you. A contract is a legal document. It is what both sides sign once the client has said yes, and it defines the binding terms.

The smoothest workflow uses both, in order: send a proposal, get a verbal or written yes, then send a contract that repeats the agreed scope and price in formal language. Many freelancers lose deals because their proposal is sloppy, and lose money because their contract is missing. You need both documents to look consistent, which is why using one tool for both saves time. We will come back to that below.

The 12 essential clauses in a freelance contract

1. Parties and contact details

Start with the full legal names of both sides (a person or a registered company), addresses and email addresses. If you work through a business entity, use the entity name. This sounds obvious, yet many disputes get complicated because it was unclear who the client actually was.

2. Project description and deliverables

Describe what you will deliver in specific, checkable terms. "Website design" is vague. "Five-page responsive website design in Figma, including home, about, services, blog and contact pages" is clear. List the file formats, quantities, word counts or page counts. The more concrete the deliverables, the easier it is to say "that is outside the scope."

3. Timeline and milestones

Include a start date, key milestones and a final delivery date. Also state what the client is responsible for: providing content, logos, access or feedback by certain dates. A useful line to add is that deadlines move if the client is late with materials or approvals. Without it, client delays quietly become your problem.

4. Fees and payment schedule

State the total price or hourly rate, the currency, and exactly when payments are due. For international clients, name the currency and who covers transfer or platform fees. A common structure is a deposit before work begins, a payment at a midpoint milestone, and a final payment on delivery. Fixed dates or clear triggers ("within 7 days of invoice") are better than "on completion."

5. Deposit and late payment terms

Many freelancers ask for 25 to 50 percent upfront from new clients, and it is reasonable to state that work does not begin until the deposit is received. Add a late fee or interest rate for overdue invoices, and say that you may pause work if payment is late. Check local rules on what late fees are allowed.

6. Revisions and change requests

Say how many rounds of revisions are included, what counts as a revision (fixing errors versus changing direction), and how extra work is priced. This single clause prevents more frustration than any other. For example: "Two rounds of revisions are included. Additional rounds or new requirements are billed at the agreed hourly rate."

7. Intellectual property and usage rights

Who owns the finished work, and when? Common approaches are that ownership transfers to the client only after full payment, or that the client receives a licence to use the work while you keep certain rights. Also state whether you may show the work in your portfolio. If your work includes third-party assets such as fonts, stock photos or plugins, clarify who pays for those licences.

8. Confidentiality

If you will see private business information, the client may ask for a confidentiality clause, and you may want one too. Keep it mutual and specific: what is confidential, how long the obligation lasts, and what is excluded (for example, information already public).

9. Independent contractor status

State that you are an independent contractor, not an employee. You control how and when you work, you use your own tools, and you are responsible for your own taxes and insurance. This matters for tax and employment classification, and the exact wording depends on your jurisdiction.

10. Termination

Describe how either side can end the agreement, the notice period, and what happens to payments for work already done. A fair clause says the client pays for all completed work and any approved expenses up to the termination date, and that deposits are non-refundable once work has started (where legally permitted).

11. Liability and warranties

Limit your liability to a reasonable amount, often the fees paid under the contract, and state that you are not responsible for indirect losses such as lost profit. Avoid promising specific business results like "10,000 new visitors" or "double your sales," because you do not control the market.

12. Governing law and dispute resolution

Say which country's or state's law governs the contract and how disputes will be handled: first a conversation, then mediation, then court or arbitration. For cross-border work this clause avoids arguments about where a dispute must be filed. Add a signature block with names, dates and, if you use one, e-signature details.

Five costly mistakes freelancers make with contracts

Copying a random template without reading it

Free templates online often contain clauses written for another country, another industry or an employer relationship. If you do not understand a clause, either learn what it means or remove it. A template you have not read is a liability.

Vague scope

Phrases like "and other tasks as needed" or "unlimited revisions" are invitations to unpaid work. Define the deliverables and define the limits.

Starting work before signing

It is tempting to begin because the client is friendly and in a hurry. But the first days of work are when you have the most leverage to get a signed agreement and a deposit. After you have delivered a draft, you have far less.

No plan for non-payment

Decide in advance what you will do if an invoice is 7, 14 and 30 days late. A polite reminder, a paused project and a formal notice each need a basis in the contract. How to write a freelance contract, step by step

  1. Gather the facts. Client name, project goal, deliverables, price, deadline and any special requirements.
  2. Start from a structured template. Use a base document with the 12 clauses above so nothing is forgotten.
  3. Customise the project-specific parts. Scope, timeline, fees and payment schedule change every time. Everything else can stay consistent.
  4. Keep the language plain. Short sentences and specific numbers beat legal-sounding filler. A contract both sides understand is easier to enforce.
  5. Get it reviewed. Have a lawyer look over your master template once, then reuse it with confidence.
  6. Send it as a clean PDF. Presentation matters. A tidy, branded document signals that you run a serious business.
  7. Collect the signature and deposit before starting. Keep a copy of the signed version somewhere safe.

Checklist before you send any contract

  • Both parties' legal names and contact details are correct
  • Deliverables are listed in specific, measurable terms
  • Start date, milestones and final deadline are written down
  • Total fee, currency and payment schedule are clear
  • Deposit and late-payment terms are stated
  • Number of revisions and price of extra work are defined
  • Ownership and portfolio rights are addressed
  • Termination and notice terms are fair to both sides
  • Governing law is named
  • Signature lines and dates are present

Frequently asked questions

Do freelancers really need a written contract?

Yes. A written contract defines scope, payment, deadlines and ownership, and gives you something concrete to point to if a client disputes the work or refuses to pay.

What is the difference between a proposal and a contract?

A proposal persuades a client to hire you by describing your solution and price. A contract is the binding agreement that sets the terms once the client says yes.

Can I use one contract template for every client?

You can reuse a solid base template, but scope, price, timeline and payment terms should be customised for each project.

How much should I ask as a deposit?

Many freelancers ask for 25 to 50 percent upfront for new clients, with the balance tied to milestones or final delivery.
