Root Rot in Houseplants: Early Signs, Causes, and How to Fix It (2026)

By the time root rot shows up on the leaves, it's already advanced underground. Here's how to catch it early, and exactly how to save the plant if you didn't.

Root rot is one of the most common houseplant killers, and also one of the most misunderstood. Most people don't notice anything is wrong until the leaves are already yellowing, wilting, or dropping — but by that point, the damage underground has usually been building for weeks. The good news is that root rot has a fairly predictable progression, and if you know what to look for above the soil, you can often catch it early enough to save the plant with a straightforward repotting.

What root rot actually is

Roots need oxygen just as much as they need water — they absorb it from air pockets in the soil. When soil stays waterlogged for too long, those air pockets fill with water instead, and the roots effectively suffocate. Dying root tissue then becomes an easy target for fungi and bacteria that are naturally present in most potting soil, and those organisms spread the rot further, turning firm white roots to brown, mushy tissue.

This is why root rot is almost always described as a symptom of overwatering rather than a separate disease on its own — the excess water creates the low-oxygen conditions that let it start, and the fungi that follow are opportunists rather than the root cause (no pun intended).

Early above-soil signs (before it's obvious)

These are the signs worth watching for before the plant looks obviously sick:

  • Soil stays wet far longer than usual after watering — this is often the very first clue, before any leaf symptom appears at all.
  • A sour, swampy smell coming from the soil, different from normal earthy potting mix smell.
  • Slower than normal growth, even though watering and light haven't changed.
  • Slight wilting even though the soil is wet — this is a key early red flag, because a plant with healthy roots wilts from dryness, but a plant with rotting roots can wilt in wet soil because the damaged roots simply can't take up water anymore.
  • Lower leaves turning a dull, slightly yellow-green rather than bright yellow, before more obvious yellowing sets in.
The single most useful early sign: a plant that wilts despite consistently moist soil. Dry-soil wilting and wet-soil wilting look almost identical from the outside, but they call for opposite fixes — which is exactly why root rot gets missed early so often.

Advanced signs

Once rot has progressed, the signs become harder to miss:

  • Yellow leaves spreading from the bottom of the plant upward
  • Leaves turning brown or black and feeling soft rather than crispy
  • Stem softening or darkening near the soil line
  • The plant becoming loose in the pot, since rotted roots no longer anchor it firmly
  • Visible mold or a white fuzzy growth on the soil surface
  • A strong, unmistakable rotten smell

How to check the roots yourself

If you suspect root rot, the only way to know for sure is to look at the roots directly:

  1. Gently remove the plant from its pot, turning it on its side and easing the root ball out rather than pulling on the stem.
  2. Brush away loose soil so you can see the roots clearly, or rinse gently under lukewarm water.
  3. Healthy roots are firm, and typically white, tan, or light cream, holding together when you handle them.
  4. Rotten roots are brown or black, feel mushy or slimy, and often the outer layer sloughs off entirely when touched, leaving a thin stringy core — a texture that's unmistakable once you've felt it.
  5. Check the whole root ball, not just the roots you can see at the surface, since rot frequently starts at the bottom and center of the pot where drainage and oxygen are worst.

Step-by-step treatment

If you've confirmed rot, act quickly — the longer damaged roots stay in wet soil, the more spreads to the remaining healthy roots.

  1. Remove the plant from its pot and gently rinse the roots clean of soil so you can see clearly what you're working with.
  2. Cut away all rotten roots with clean, sharp scissors or pruning shears, cutting back to firm white or tan tissue. Don't leave any brown, mushy sections, since they'll continue spreading rot to what's left.
  3. Sanitize your tools between cuts with rubbing alcohol if the rot looks advanced, to avoid spreading fungus to healthy tissue.
  4. Trim back some top growth to match the reduced root system — a plant with fewer roots can't support as many leaves, and trimming reduces the stress of trying to.
  5. Repot in fresh, well-draining potting mix in a clean pot with a drainage hole. Never reuse the old soil, since it likely still harbors the fungi responsible.
  6. Water lightly for the first couple of weeks rather than resuming a normal schedule immediately, giving the reduced root system time to recover before it needs to support full watering again.
  7. Watch closely for the following few weeks — some leaf loss during recovery is normal as the plant sheds what the reduced roots can no longer support, and isn't necessarily a sign of failure.
If there are no healthy roots left — everything is brown and mushy with no firm white tissue anywhere — full recovery usually isn't possible. If any healthy stem or leaf sections remain, taking cuttings to propagate separately is often a better use of what's left than trying to save the original root system.

Preventing it from coming back

  • Always use pots with drainage holes, and empty any saucer underneath after watering rather than letting it sit full.
  • Check soil moisture before watering rather than following a fixed schedule — different seasons, humidity levels, and pot sizes all change how fast soil dries.
  • Choose a well-draining potting mix appropriate to the plant, adding perlite or orchid bark for species that need extra drainage.
  • Size the pot to the plant — going up only 1–2 inches (2–5 cm) in diameter when repotting avoids excess soil volume that stays wet too long.
  • Water less in winter or any period of slower growth, when the plant is using water more slowly than usual.

FAQ

Can a plant survive root rot?

Yes, if caught while some healthy white roots remain. The plant needs enough healthy root mass left to support itself after the rotten parts are removed. If the entire root system has turned to mush with no firm roots left, recovery usually isn't possible, though healthy stem cuttings can sometimes be propagated separately.

What does a healthy root look like versus a rotten one?

Healthy roots are firm and typically white, cream, or light tan, holding together when handled. Rotten roots turn brown or black, feel mushy or slimy, and the outer layer often slides off in your fingers, leaving a thin stringy core — a very distinctive texture once you've felt it.

Should I water less if I suspect root rot?

Reducing water alone won't fix existing rot, since the damage is already in the soil and on the roots. The soil needs replacing and the dead roots need removing. Reducing water going forward, and confirming the pot has drainage, does help prevent it from returning after treatment.

How long does it take to recover from root rot?

Most plants show renewed growth within 2 to 6 weeks after treatment if enough healthy root remained, though the plant often looks worse for the first week or two as it drops stressed leaves before recovering. Full recovery to the plant's original size can take several months.
