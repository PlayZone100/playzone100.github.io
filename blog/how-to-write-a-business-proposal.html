How to Write a Business Proposal: A Step-by-Step Guide (2026)

Updated September 20, 2026 · 12-minute read

A business proposal is often the moment a deal is won or lost. The client has talked to you, maybe to two or three competitors, and now they are comparing documents. The one that is clearest, most relevant and easiest to say yes to usually wins, and it is not always the cheapest.

This guide shows you how to write a business proposal from scratch: what to include, in what order, how to word each section, how to present your price, and which mistakes make clients lose interest. It works for freelancers, consultants, agencies and small businesses selling services to other businesses.

What is a business proposal?

A business proposal is a document that offers a specific solution to a specific client's problem, for a specific price and timeline. Its job is to persuade. It is different from a contract, which is the binding agreement signed after the client agrees. If you want the legal side, read our guide to the freelance contract template and its 12 essential clauses.

It is also different from a quote or estimate. A quote says "this costs X." A proposal says "here is what we understood about your situation, here is how we will solve it, here is what you will get, and here is what it costs." That extra context is what lets you charge for value instead of competing only on price.

Two types of proposals

Solicited proposals

The client asks for it, sometimes through a formal request for proposal (RFP), sometimes through an email saying "send us an offer." You already know they have a need and a budget in mind. Follow any instructions they give exactly, including format, length and deadlines.

Unsolicited proposals

You reach out with an idea before the client has asked. These are harder to win because you must first prove the problem exists and matters. Lead with a short, specific observation about their business, for example a slow checkout page or an unclear onboarding email, and offer a concrete fix.

The 10 sections of a winning business proposal

1. Title page and cover note

Include the project name, the client's name, your name or company, the date and your contact details. Add a short cover note, three or four sentences, thanking them for the conversation and stating what the document contains. Small touches like the client's correct name and logo show care.

2. Executive summary

This is the most-read part of your proposal, and some decision makers read nothing else. In one short paragraph, state the client's goal, your proposed solution, the timeline and the investment. Write it last, after everything else is done, so it is accurate.

Example: "You want to launch a new online store before the holiday season and need product pages that convert. We propose a four-week design and build project, delivered in two milestones, for a fixed fee of $4,800."

3. Understanding of the problem

Show that you listened. Restate the client's situation in their own words: what they are trying to achieve, what is in the way and what success looks like. Use details from your discovery call. Generic openings like "We are a leading provider of solutions" tell the client nothing. A precise problem statement makes them think, "They get it."

4. Proposed solution

Explain what you will do and why that approach fits. Focus on outcomes, not just tasks. "We will rewrite your product descriptions" is a task. "We will rewrite your product descriptions to answer the questions customers ask before buying, so fewer visitors leave to search elsewhere" is an outcome. Keep technical language proportional to your reader.

5. Scope and deliverables

List exactly what is included, in specific terms: number of pages, formats, rounds of revision, meetings. Then list what is not included. Writing out exclusions is one of the simplest ways to prevent scope creep later, and it flows naturally into the contract that follows.

6. Timeline and milestones

Give a start date, key milestones and a delivery date. A short table or list works well: Week 1 discovery and outline, Week 2 first draft, Week 3 revisions, Week 4 final delivery. State what you need from the client and by when, because delays on their side affect the schedule.

7. Pricing and payment terms

Show the investment clearly. You can present a single fixed price, a breakdown by phase, or two or three package options. Options are powerful: they shift the client's question from "should I hire them?" to "which option should I choose?" Include the currency, what is covered, payment schedule, and how long the price is valid. 8. About you and proof

Now, and only now, talk about yourself. Two or three short paragraphs on relevant experience, plus proof: a brief case study with numbers, a testimonial, or a link to similar work. Choose evidence that matches this client's industry or problem. One strong, relevant example beats a long list of unrelated logos.

9. Terms and conditions

Summarise the key terms: deposit, revisions, ownership of the work, confidentiality, cancellation. Keep it short in the proposal and refer to the full contract for details. This avoids surprises and prepares the client for the next step.

10. Next steps and call to action

Tell the client exactly what to do to say yes: reply to the email, sign on this page, or book a kickoff call. Add an expiry date for the offer, for example "This proposal is valid until October 15." A clear next step removes friction, and a reasonable deadline creates gentle momentum.

How to write a proposal, step by step

  1. Do real discovery. Ask about goals, budget range, deadlines, past attempts and who makes the decision. The better your questions, the more specific your proposal.
  2. Choose your angle. Decide the one main reason this client should pick you: speed, expertise in their niche, a proven process, or better value.
  3. Outline before writing. Use the 10 sections above as your skeleton and fill in bullet points first.
  4. Write in the client's language. Reuse their words for their problems. Avoid jargon they did not use.
  5. Price with confidence. Present the number plainly, without apologies, after the value is clear.
  6. Design for skimming. Use clear headings, short paragraphs, and a clean layout. Decision makers scan first and read second.
  7. Proofread and check the details. Names, dates, totals and links must be right. A typo in the client's company name costs trust.
  8. Send it as a PDF or link with a short email. Mention the key benefit in the email and offer a call to answer questions.

Writing tips that make proposals more persuasive

  • Lead with them, not you. The words "you" and "your" should appear far more than "we" and "our."
  • Be specific. "Reduce support emails" is vague. "Reduce repetitive support emails by answering the five most common questions on a help page" is believable.
  • Quantify where honest. Use real numbers from past projects, and never promise results you do not control.
  • Anticipate objections. If the client may worry about timing or risk, address it directly with a plan or a guarantee you can actually keep.
  • Keep it as short as the project allows. Two to six pages is enough for most small and medium projects.

Common business proposal mistakes

Sending a generic template with only the name changed

Clients can tell. A template is a starting point for structure and formatting; the problem statement and solution must be written for the client.

Burying the price or hiding it

Making the client search for the cost creates suspicion. Show it clearly, framed by the value delivered.

Talking too much about yourself

Your history matters less than their outcome. Keep the "about us" section short and relevant.

Vague scope

Unclear deliverables lead to arguments later. Specify what is included and excluded, then carry the same wording into your contract.

No follow-up

Many deals are won by the person who follows up politely. Send a short message two to four business days later, and another a week after that if there is silence. Ask whether they have questions rather than whether they have "decided yet."

After the yes: from proposal to contract to invoice

When the client accepts, move quickly. Send the contract that repeats the agreed scope, price and timeline, and collect the signature and deposit before starting. Business proposal checklist

  • Executive summary states goal, solution, timeline and price
  • Client's problem is described in specific terms
  • Deliverables and exclusions are clearly listed
  • Timeline with milestones and client responsibilities
  • Price, currency, payment schedule and validity date
  • One relevant proof point or case study
  • Key terms summarised, with the contract to follow
  • A clear next step and an expiry date
  • Names, numbers and links proofread

Frequently asked questions

How long should a business proposal be?

Most small-project proposals work best at two to six pages. Longer, complex projects may need more, but every section should help the client decide.

What is the difference between a proposal and a quote?

A quote lists a price for defined items. A proposal explains the problem, your solution, the process, the timeline and the price, and is designed to persuade.

Should I include pricing in a proposal?

Yes. Clients expect to see a clear price or price range, ideally presented after you have shown the value of the work.

How long should I wait before following up on a proposal?

A polite follow-up two to four business days after sending is common, followed by a second one a week later if there is no reply.

Final thoughts

A good proposal is not about impressive words. It is about showing the client that you understand their problem, have a clear plan, and make the decision easy.
