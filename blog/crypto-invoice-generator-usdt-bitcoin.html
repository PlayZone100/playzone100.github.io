Why generic invoicing tools handle crypto badly

Standard invoice software is built around fiat currency: two decimal places, one symbol, one payment method. Crypto doesn't fit that shape. Bitcoin and Ethereum amounts commonly need up to eight decimal places. USDT alone exists on multiple blockchain networks. And most tools give you a single generic "payment details" text box rather than a proper wallet field — which is how mislabeled or mistyped addresses happen.

Invoice Studio was built with crypto as a first-class payment type, not a workaround: BTC, ETH, USDT, USDC, SOL and XMR each get correct decimal precision, and wallet lines are a dedicated field, not free text.

Rule one: name the network, not just the coin

This is the single most common source of lost crypto payments. USDT exists on Ethereum (ERC20), Tron (TRC20), and other chains. Sending USDT to a TRC20 address using the ERC20 network — or the reverse — can permanently lose the funds. Every wallet line on an invoice should say the network explicitly:

  • Correct: USDT (TRC20) — TR7N...8x2Q
  • Risky: USDT — TR7N...8x2Q

Invoice Studio labels every wallet line with its network by default, so this isn't something you have to remember to type correctly each time.

Pricing in a stablecoin vs quoting a floating rate

You have two honest options for pricing crypto invoices:

  • Bill in a stablecoin (USDT or USDC). The number on the invoice is the number your client sends — no exchange-rate ambiguity for either side.
  • Bill in your normal currency, state the crypto amount separately. Useful if your contract is denominated in USD or EUR but you're being paid in BTC or ETH. Note the rate and the date it was calculated, since crypto prices move.

Invoice Studio doesn't fetch live exchange rates — it stays fully offline by design — so you set the amount yourself and stay in control of which rate you quote.

Letting your client copy the address correctly

A wallet address is 26 to 42-plus characters of case-sensitive text. Asking a client to retype it from a PDF invites a single mistyped character to send funds somewhere unrecoverable. In Invoice Studio, wallet addresses print as selectable text directly in the PDF, so a client copies the exact string instead of transcribing it by hand.
