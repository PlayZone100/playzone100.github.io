Why multi-currency is harder than swapping a symbol

Formatting currency correctly is more than swapping "$" for "€". Different currencies use different decimal conventions — the Japanese yen has no minor unit, most currencies use two decimals, and crypto assets like Bitcoin commonly need up to eight. A generator that just relabels the symbol on a fixed two-decimal template produces invoices that look wrong to clients used to their own currency's formatting.

Invoice Studio formats each of its 150+ currencies the way its own market expects, including proper decimal precision for crypto rather than squeezing everything into two places.

Billing in the client's currency vs. your own

There are two common approaches:

  • Invoice in your own currency and let the client's bank handle conversion — simplest for your own bookkeeping, but the client sees an unfamiliar amount and conversion fee.
  • Invoice in the client's local currency — friendlier for them, but you're exposed to exchange-rate movement between issuing the invoice and being paid.

Neither is universally correct; it depends on your contract and how comfortable you are absorbing exchange-rate risk. What matters is that your tool supports whichever you choose without a plan-tier restriction.

Crypto as a currency, not an add-on

If you invoice in USDT, Bitcoin or other crypto, that's effectively another currency choice, and it deserves the same first-class treatment as USD or EUR: correct decimals, a proper wallet address field, and the network labeled explicitly. Tax across currencies

Tax rates are set by jurisdiction, not currency, so your invoicing tool needs to let tax and currency vary independently — a client billed in EUR under one VAT rate, another billed in AED with no tax, from the same tool without switching modes. Invoice Studio handles tax per invoice: no tax, one flat rate, or per line item, with prices set as tax-inclusive or exclusive as needed.
