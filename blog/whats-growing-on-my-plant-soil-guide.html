What's Growing on My Plant's Soil? Mold, Mushrooms, Algae and More Explained (2026)

White fuzz, tiny mushrooms, a green film, a crusty layer, or something that's actually moving — here's a field guide to everything that shows up on potting soil, what it means, and whether to worry.

Updated September 2026 · 14 minute read

Jump straight to what you're seeing:

🤍 Fuzzy white mold 🍄 Small mushrooms 🟢 Green film or algae 🧂 Crusty white layer 🌱 Moss 🪱 Tiny white worms ⚫ Black sooty coating 🐜 Ants in the soil

Almost everything that shows up on the surface of houseplant soil falls into one of eight categories, and most of them are more cosmetic than dangerous — potting soil is a living environment, and a healthy one usually has some biological activity in it. That said, a few of these are worth taking seriously because of what they signal about watering or plant health, even when the growth itself isn't harmful. Here's how to tell them apart and what, if anything, to do about each.

🤍 Fuzzy white mold

Mostly harmless, but a warning sign

This is the most commonly reported soil issue, and it looks exactly like what it is: a fuzzy, cottony, sometimes web-like white growth spreading across the soil surface, most often appearing within a day or two of watering.

What it is: saprophytic fungi — organisms that feed on decaying organic matter in the soil, such as bits of bark, peat, or old roots in the potting mix. They thrive specifically in damp, poorly ventilated conditions.

What to do:

  • Scrape off the visible mold from the top layer of soil and discard it.
  • Let the soil dry out more than usual before the next watering, and check that the pot has proper drainage.
  • Improve airflow around the plant — a closed, humid corner with no air movement is exactly the environment this mold prefers.
  • If it keeps returning quickly, consider top-dressing with a thin layer of cinnamon (which has natural antifungal properties) or switching to a slightly faster-draining soil mix.

🍄 Small mushrooms

Generally harmless to the plant

Finding an actual mushroom growing out of a houseplant pot catches most people off guard, but it's a fairly common and mostly benign occurrence, especially in soil mixes containing bark or other slow-decaying organic material.

What it is: the visible fruiting body of a fungal network already living in the soil, usually feeding on decomposing organic matter rather than the plant's living roots. The fungus itself was likely already there — you're just now seeing it fruit, typically triggered by a period of consistent moisture.

What to do:

  • Remove the mushroom if you'd rather not look at it, but it isn't harming the plant and doesn't strictly need removing.
  • If pets or small children are around, remove mushrooms promptly regardless, since some varieties that grow in houseplant soil can cause stomach upset if eaten, even if they aren't a threat to the plant itself.
  • No treatment is needed for the plant's sake — this isn't a sign of disease or poor plant health.

🟢 Green film or algae

Cosmetic, not harmful

A thin, slippery, or slightly fuzzy green coating on the soil surface — sometimes also on the outside of a porous pot — is almost always algae rather than anything plant-related.

What it is: algae need moisture and light to grow, and a soil surface that stays damp for extended periods in a bright spot provides exactly that combination.

What to do:

  • Scrape off the top layer if you want it gone, though it will likely return if conditions don't change.
  • Let the soil surface dry out a bit more between waterings, since consistently damp topsoil is the main driver.
  • A thin layer of decorative gravel or pebbles on the soil surface can reduce visible algae by blocking light to the moist layer underneath.

🧂 Crusty white layer

Sign of fertilizer buildup

Easy to confuse with mold at a glance, but the texture and cause are completely different — this one is mineral, not biological.

What it is: as covered in more depth in our brown leaves guide, this is dried mineral salt residue from fertilizer or tap water minerals, left behind as water evaporates from the soil surface. It's hard, chalky, and often concentrated near the pot rim or drainage holes rather than spread evenly like mold.

What to do:

  • Flush the soil thoroughly with plain water, letting it drain fully, to wash out accumulated salts.
  • Scrape off the visible crust from the surface rather than letting it wash back into the soil.
  • Reduce fertilizer strength or frequency going forward, and skip feeding entirely during dormant seasons.

🌱 Moss

Harmless, sometimes even desired

A soft, low, green carpet-like growth on the soil surface is moss, and it's actually intentionally cultivated by some plant keepers for a natural, terrarium-like look.

What it is: moss thrives on consistently moist, undisturbed soil surfaces, similar to the conditions algae likes but with a different organism growing in.

What to do: nothing is required — it's not harmful to the plant. Remove it if you prefer a cleaner look, or leave it if you like the appearance; either choice is fine for the plant's health.

🪱 Tiny white worms or larvae

Usually fungus gnat larvae

Small, thin, white or translucent larvae wriggling in the top layer of soil, especially visible right after watering, are almost always fungus gnat larvae rather than a more serious pest.

What it is: as covered in our pest identification guide, fungus gnats lay eggs in consistently moist soil, and the larvae feed primarily on decaying organic matter, occasionally nibbling on fine root hairs in seedlings or very young plants.

What to do:

  • Let the top inch or two (2-5 cm) of soil dry out between waterings, since the larvae need consistent moisture to survive.
  • Use yellow sticky traps to catch the adult flies and interrupt the breeding cycle.
  • A thin layer of sand on the soil surface makes it harder for adults to lay new eggs.

⚫ Black sooty coating

Sign of a pest problem, not soil issue

Unlike the other items on this list, a black, sooty film is usually not a soil problem at all, but a downstream sign of a pest infestation on the plant above it.

What it is: sooty mold grows on the sugary "honeydew" excretion left behind by aphids, mealybugs, or scale insects feeding on the plant. The mold itself grows on the honeydew, wherever it drips — often the soil surface below an infested plant.

What to do:

  • Check the plant itself for the pests described in our pest guide — treating the actual infestation is what stops the sooty mold from returning.
  • Wipe off visible sooty residue from soil and leaves with a damp cloth once the underlying pest is under control.

🐜 Ants in the soil

Usually a symptom, not the core problem

Ants occasionally nesting in a houseplant pot are rarely feeding on the plant directly — they're typically after the honeydew from aphids or mealybugs (much like the sooty mold above), or simply using dry, undisturbed soil as convenient nesting material.

What to do:

  • Check the plant for aphids, mealybugs, or scale first, since ants are frequently a clue pointing to one of these rather than a problem on their own.
  • If there's no pest present and it's simply a nesting site, soaking the soil thoroughly (a full watering that saturates the pot) often encourages ants to relocate on their own.
  • Persistent nests can be treated with a plant-safe ant bait placed near, not in, the pot.

FAQ

Is white mold on plant soil dangerous to my plant?

Surface mold on potting soil is rarely dangerous to a healthy plant directly, and is mostly a sign that the soil is staying too wet or has poor airflow. It can become a bigger concern for people with mold allergies or sensitivities in the home, or if it signals overwatering severe enough to also be affecting the roots.

Should I remove mushrooms growing in my houseplant pot?

You can remove them for appearance, but they generally aren't harming the plant. Mushrooms are the visible fruiting body of fungi already living in the soil, usually feeding on decaying organic matter, and their presence isn't typically a sign of a problem with the plant itself.

Is the green film on my plant's soil harmful?

The green film is usually algae, which isn't harmful to the plant. It grows because the soil surface is staying consistently damp and getting some light, the same combination algae needs anywhere. It's mostly a cosmetic issue and a sign to let the surface dry out a bit more between waterings.

How do I tell the difference between mold and dried fertilizer salt on soil?

Mold usually looks fuzzy, cottony, or web-like and can appear or spread within a day or two. Fertilizer salt buildup looks like a hard, crusty, chalky white or yellowish layer, often concentrated near the pot's edges or around the drainage holes, and doesn't have the soft or fuzzy texture mold has.
